## Direction AB — the charter

**The configuration is a document.** Settings pages are an artefact
of databases, not of understanding: what an operator wants to know is
"how is this network kept?", and the natural form of the answer is a
literate text. The charter writes the whole configuration as
articles — who may look in · which machines may speak · what is
watched for · what is kept · what the charter does not govern · the
server's own clause — with every editable value a **term in a
sentence**.

- **Amending in place**: a term opens where it stands; the popover
  offers only the values the sentence could truthfully end with;
  the paragraph never moves.
- **The margin is the audit log**: amendments live beside the
  article they changed, so "why is off-hours paused?" is answered by
  the note next to the clause.
- **The secret is a sentence that expires**: the mint writes itself
  into article II in the document's own voice, amber because it is
  a fact about now, then gone.
- **The viewer gets the native form**: a document is already
  read-only — kai reads the same charter, margin and all; the
  admin's dashed underline is the only ink that leaves the page.
- **Honesty as articles**: what is configured in config.yaml says
  so; the estate's edges and the API clause are articles V and VI,
  written where every reader meets them.
- Cost, stated: prose templates need care per fact-shape (and per
  locale if that ever comes); one charter view composed over the
  existing GETs; terms map 1:1 onto existing write endpoints; no
  new stores.

## Direction AC — the engine room

**Settings live on the machine.** A setting is meaningless apart
from the machinery it governs, so stop filing settings by noun and
draw the machine: mikroview's signal path as a live vertical
diagram — the direction the app already reads — door (syslog
listener) · store · watchers · flags desk · heralds, with the two
**side doors** (who may look in · which machines may speak) beside
the path, because they govern entry, not flow.

- **Every number is arrived traffic**: 7.4/s at the door, 41 208 in
  the store, 6 on the desk — the room doubles as the status page,
  and a setting is read against what it governs in the same glance.
- **Opening a station is zooming, not navigating**: the watchers'
  bench unfolds between the store and the desk, path dimmed but
  visible; a key is minted at the door it opens.
- **Honesty about reach**: stations the UI cannot amend (the door's
  ports, the heralds) say "set in config.yaml — the room shows what
  is, the file decides"; no knob is drawn that does not exist.
- **The viewer's room is the room at rest**: same machine, same live
  truth, the admin's dashed ink gone, values remaining as bold facts
  in the stations' sentences — the machine observed rather than
  adjusted, which is the product's own ethic applied to itself.
- **The room teaches the product**: reading the path once tells a
  new user mikroview's architecture and manners (the router speaks
  first; nothing is probed; word goes out only by the heralds).
- Cost, stated: the room must stay a diagram, not become a
  dashboard — its numbers are context for knobs, never charts; one
  room view over the existing GETs; no new stores.

## The choice they pose

The charter reads best and is cheapest to build; the engine room
teaches best and doubles as status. They agree on all of Z's bones
and differ in the organising idea: **meaning in prose** versus
**place in the machine**. (They could even coexist — the room as
the page, the charter as its printable export — a round-3 question,
not a claim.)
