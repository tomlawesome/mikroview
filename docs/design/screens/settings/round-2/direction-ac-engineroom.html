The room is also the status page. Every number on it is the hour's real, arrived traffic — 7.4/s at the door, 41 208 in the store, 6 on the desk. A setting is read against what it is actually governing, in the same glance.
Stop filing settings by noun; draw the machine. The signal path runs top to bottom — the direction this app reads — from the router's push to the heralds, and every setting lives on the station it governs. The side doors (people, machines) stand beside the path because that is where they stand in reality: they govern entry, not flow. Configuring the thing teaches how the thing works.
Where a knob lives is information. "Retention" filed under a Settings page is a word; 72 hours written on the store, between the door and the watchers, explains itself — what it holds back, who reads from it, what falls off the end. The diagram is the documentation.
Honesty about reach. Stations the UI cannot amend (the door's ports, the heralds) say "set in config.yaml — the room shows what is, the file decides" — the config-problems banner's remediation posture, promoted to a design principle. No knob is drawn that does not exist.
Z's build facts inherited. Dashed-underline knobs are the admin's ink; writes 403 server-side; the GETs behind each station widened deliberately, one matrix row each; the flow pulse is the rail's receiving pulse, instant under reduced motion.

Doors govern entry; the path governs flow. The room's two kinds of settings never mix: what comes in (people, machines) is amended at the doors, what happens to it (keep, watch, flag, tell) is amended on the path. That split is the system's real architecture — the UI just stops hiding it.
Setup joins the room honestly. "Run setup…" remains the workshop off the engine room — it mints the first ingest key and prints router commands for the operator to run — admin-only outright, per the ratified rail. The room links to it from the door station; it never inlines it.

The engine room · what a viewer learns here

Why a viewer gets the whole room. Kai's real questions — is ingest alive, what is watching tonight, why are quiet hours quiet, how long does the log go back, who do I ask — are each a number or a sentence on the station where it belongs. The room answers them in one glance, in the machine's own shape, which no per-noun settings page ever did.
The room teaches the product. A new viewer who reads the path once — door, store, watchers, desk, heralds — has been told mikroview's whole architecture, including its manners: the router speaks first, nothing is probed, word goes out only by the heralds. The settings surface doubles as the missing "how it works" page.
Keyboard and announcement. The path is a list landmark read top to bottom; Enter opens a station (zoom, not navigation); Tab walks knobs (admins) or stations (viewers); the chip is read once as the page's description. The flow pulse is decorative and silent.
Read-only is the room at rest. The engine room's viewer form is not a stripped page — it is the machine observed rather than adjusted, which is what mikroview itself does to the network all day. The product's own ethic, applied to its own controls.
AB and AC agree on the bones. Both wild passes keep Z's build facts (worded states, 403 writes, deliberate GET widening, secrets in no GET), the one-admin console rule, and Run setup… as the admin's workshop. They differ in the organising idea: the charter files everything by meaning in prose; the engine room files everything by place in the machine.
The choice they pose. The charter reads best and is cheapest to build (prose over existing GETs); the engine room teaches best and doubles as status. Both could even coexist — the room as the page, the charter as its printable "how is this network kept?" export — but that is a round-3 question, not a claim.
